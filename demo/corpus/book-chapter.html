Chapter FourThe Cartography of Rivers

A river is never a line. It is a negotiation between the water and the land, renewed every season, and any map that draws it as a single stroke is telling a convenient lie. The cartographer's problem is therefore not to find the river's edge but to decide which of its many edges, at which moment, deserves to be called the river itself.This chapter follows the convention established in section 4.2, where the thalweg — the line of deepest channel — is taken as the working centre.

4.1 The problem of the bank

The bank of a river is a fiction of convenience. On a slow meander the water may creep half a metre up the slope in a wet spring and retreat again by midsummer, so a fixed line surveyed in March is wrong by August. Early surveyors solved this by surveying twice a year and publishing both lines, but a map with two banks for one river confuses the very readers it means to help. The compromise that survived is the ordinary high-water mark, a legal as much as a physical boundary, and it is the line that most national mapping agencies still use.The high-water mark is defined in most jurisdictions by statute rather than by measurement, which is why two neighbouring countries can disagree about a bank that neither disputes.

Where the bank is a cliff the problem disappears; where it is a marsh it becomes acute. The cartographer resolves the marsh by drawing a single braided band and letting the reader understand that the band, not its edges, is the river. The technique is honest in a way that a single line is not, because it admits the uncertainty instead of hiding it.

A map that cannot show doubt is a map that cannot be trusted. The braided band, the dashed reach, the question mark beside an inferred tributary — these are the cartographer's admissions of humility, and they are more valuable than any amount of false precision.

4.2 The thalweg and the centre line

The thalweg is the line joining the deepest points of successive cross-sections, and for navigation it is the only line that matters. A vessel follows the deep water, not the geometric middle, and where a river runs against one bank the channel may lie wholly on that side. Treaties that fix a boundary "along the middle of the river" therefore tend to be read, in practice, as "along the thalweg", which is a different line altogether.The distinction matters most on meandering rivers, where the geometric middle can shift by hundreds of metres while the thalweg stays within a few tens. The map that shows both, the middle as a fine rule and the thalweg as a heavier line, teaches the reader more in one glance than a page of explanation.

For the inland navigator the practical consequence is simple: follow the deep water, watch the sounding, and treat the printed centre line as a guide rather than a promise. The chart is a hypothesis about the river, and the river has the final word.

4.3 Confluence and the naming of rivers

At a confluence two rivers become one, and the cartographer must decide which name the combined river carries. The rule of the larger discharge is the most defensible, but it is not the rule most rivers follow in common usage; the name that survives is usually the one that was applied first, or the one attached to the larger settlement on its bank. Thus the cartographer records a name that history chose, not one that hydrology would recommend.The classic case is the confluence of the Missouri and the Mississippi: by discharge the Missouri is the larger, yet the combined river carries the Mississippi's name.

The convention is recorded rather than corrected because a map is read by people who have a stake in the names. A cartographer who renames a river on purely scientific grounds produces a document that is correct and unusable at the same time. The resolution is to print the received name and note the discharge figures in the margin, so the reader can see both the convention and the fact behind it.
