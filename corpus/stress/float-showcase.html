Setting Type Beside the Flow

A page of prose is a single stream: one paragraph follows the next, line after line, from the first word to the last. Print rarely stays that simple. A figure needs to sit beside its reference, a quotation deserves to be lifted out of the body, a sidebar carries a detail too long for a footnote. In CSS these intrusions are floats — boxes drawn out of the running text and set against the margin, with the body wrapping around them like water around a stone. This fixture exercises that machinery: a floated figure on the right, a pull quote on the left, and justified text flowing around both.

Figure 1 — A floated panel

In print, a figure or table is lifted out of the running text and set beside it. The float is measured first, at its declared width; the body text is then broken into segments, each segment shaped by the intrusion beside it, until the float's bottom is passed and the column widens again.

Fig. 1 — A floated panel with its caption, set at 2 inches.

How does an engine place such a box? The float is removed from the in-flow cursor entirely: it does not advance the paragraph below it, and the next in-flow line begins at the same height where the float began. A right float sits flush against the right content edge, a left float against the left edge, each offset by its own margin. Its width comes from the style sheet when one is declared, or from the widest line of its content when the width is left automatic — the same measurement the engine uses for a table column, capped at the inner width of the page.

A float is a parallel flow: it advances its own cursor while the text beside it advances its own.

Once the float is placed, the body text must wrap around it. The engine does not break a whole paragraph once at the full column width and hope for the best. It walks the text in segments, recomputing the available width at the start of each one: a line whose band overlaps a left float is indented by that float's width, a line beside a right float is shortened from the margin side, and a line below every float's bottom returns to the full measure. The segment ends when the line crosses a float's top or bottom — when the intrusion set changes — or when the page runs out.

Fragmentation is where this design earns its keep. A float that is taller than the space remaining on the page does not shove the paragraph aside. It suspends at the page boundary and resumes at the top of the next page, while the in-flow text continues beside it in the current page — two parallel flows, each advancing its own cursor, meeting again on the next page where the text wraps around the float's continuation. The text resume is anchored by consumed source characters, not by line counts, so the break is exact even when the available width differs between pages.

What the reader sees is the outcome of that arithmetic: a page where the body never collides with a figure, where a pull quote sits in the white space of a paragraph rather than punching a hole in it, and where the margins stay clean from the first page to the last. The figure below the quote demonstrates the same rules with the float on the left, proving the machinery is symmetric. Between the two intrusions the text narrows, widens, and narrows again, and every line in between is justified to the exact measure the float allows.

What the fixture measures

This document is the first corpus fixture to exercise CSS floats, and the first time the demo scoreboard can move on the float feature line. Prince 16.2 and TypeAnvil render the same HTML with the same page geometry; the comparison records page counts and per-page pixel differences. Where the two engines disagree — a float placed a line higher or lower, a caption wrapped differently at the same width — the difference is data, not noise, and the gallery shows it side by side.

Fixture exercises CORE-62 floats: right-float figure with explicit width, left-float pull quote, segment-based text wrap, and justified body text around both. No clear is used (deferred in the floats spec). line-height 1.4 pinned per corpus convention; residual line-box divergence tracked as CORE-90.
